How to Actually Study — Not Just Reread Your Notes

By The Pulse Founder  ·  5 min read

📚

I used to study by reading my notes over and over until they felt familiar, then walking into the test confident and walking out confused. It took me way too long to figure out that the familiar feeling was the problem — not the proof that I'd learned anything.

Psychologists call it the "illusion of fluency." When you reread something, it gets easier to read, and your brain reads that ease as "I know this." But recognizing information when it's in front of you is completely different from being able to pull it out of your head when the page is blank. The test asks you to pull it out. Rereading never practices that.

"The single most effective study technique in the research isn't rereading or highlighting — it's retrieval practice: closing the book and forcing yourself to recall the answer from memory."

The fix is almost annoyingly simple: close your notes and try to write down everything you remember, or answer questions from memory before you check. It feels worse than rereading because it's harder and you get things wrong. That difficulty is the entire point — every time you struggle to recall something and then find it, you're strengthening the exact pathway the test will use. Studies that compare the two aren't close. Students who quiz themselves consistently outperform students who reread, even when the rereaders spend more time.

The second thing that changed everything for me was spacing. Cramming five hours the night before a test technically gets information into your head, but it leaves almost as fast as it arrived. Spreading that same five hours across a week — even just twenty minutes a day — lets the memory rebuild each time it starts to fade, which is what makes it stick for more than 48 hours. Same total effort, wildly different results.

So here's the whole system, and it's boring on purpose: don't reread — quiz yourself. Don't cram — space it out. Get things wrong on your own terms before the test does it for you. It's less comfortable than highlighting a page pink, and it works about ten times better. The goal of studying was never to feel ready. It was to actually be ready.
