How to Start Making Real Money Before You Graduate

By The Pulse Founder  ·  6 min read

💰

The advice most adults give teenagers about money is bad. Not wrong — just about fifteen years too late. "Open a savings account." "Start investing early." "Get a part-time job." These are fine as far as they go, but they skip the part where you actually have money to save or invest, and they assume a work situation that doesn't fit most high schoolers' lives.

What I actually wanted to know was: how do I start generating income before I'm 18, without needing someone to hand me a W-2? And after spending a few months researching this, talking to teen founders and reading everything I could find on the subject, the answer turns out to be pretty clear — and almost nobody talks about it directly.

"The teens who make real money in high school aren't the ones who worked the most hours at minimum wage. They're the ones who identified something they could do better than most adults in a narrow area, and sold that skill directly."

Service businesses are the lowest-barrier path to income that exists. If you can mow lawns, clean cars, walk dogs, edit videos, design graphics, build websites, tutor in a subject, or photograph events — you have a product. The only thing standing between that skill and money is telling people it exists. A $20 flyer in the right neighborhood, or a few direct messages to local businesses on Instagram, will get you your first customer faster than any app or platform ever will.

The second path is digital. Creating and selling something — a Notion template, a study guide, a custom graphic pack — is a one-time effort that can generate income indefinitely. Platforms like Gumroad make it possible to sell a digital product in an afternoon with no business license, no bank account required, and no minimum age. A high schooler who creates a well-designed AP Biology study pack and prices it at $5 can realistically sell it to hundreds of students per year through nothing but TikTok and Reddit posts.

The third path is the one most people overlook: learning to sell other people's things. Flipping — finding underpriced items at garage sales, thrift stores, or Facebook Marketplace and reselling them at a profit — is a genuine business skill dressed up as a side hustle. The margins on furniture, electronics, and sneakers can be significant. More importantly, it teaches you how to spot value before other people do, which is probably the most transferable business skill there is.

None of this requires a trust fund, a car, or parental permission beyond basic common sense. What it requires is showing up consistently. The high schoolers who fail at making money usually fail in the first week when no one responds immediately. The ones who succeed just kept going — a second flyer, a second post, one more direct message. Income compounds the same way interest does. The early returns are small. They stop being small faster than you'd expect.
