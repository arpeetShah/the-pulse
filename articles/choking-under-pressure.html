How I Stopped Choking Under Pressure

By The Pulse Founder  ·  4 min read

🎾

There's a specific kind of loss that stays with you. Not the ones where you got outplayed — the ones where you had the point, the game, the match, and your own body seemed to hand it back. Tight arm, short breath, a serve that suddenly forgot how to land. For a while I thought that meant I wasn't tough enough. It turns out I just didn't understand what was actually happening.

Choking isn't a lack of skill and it isn't weakness. It's your nervous system doing exactly what it's built to do — flooding you with adrenaline when it senses something important. The problem is that the same response that helps you sprint from danger is terrible for anything requiring fine control. Your heart rate spikes, your attention narrows, and you start consciously micromanaging movements that normally run on autopilot. That's the real killer: thinking too hard about a swing you've done ten thousand times.

"Pressure doesn't make you worse by taking your skill away. It makes you worse by making you pay attention to skills that work best when you leave them alone."

What actually helped me wasn't trying to feel calm — that never works, because you can't talk your body out of adrenaline. What worked was giving my mind a job so it couldn't interfere. Before every serve I started doing the exact same routine: same number of bounces, one slow breath, one target. Not a superstition — a way of occupying the part of my brain that wanted to panic, so my body could just do the thing it already knew.

The second shift was how I read the nerves themselves. Sports psychologists have found that athletes who tell themselves "I'm excited" under pressure perform better than ones who tell themselves "calm down," because the physical feeling of excitement and anxiety is nearly identical — the only difference is the story you attach to it. Now when my heart races before a big point, I try to take it as a sign that I care, not a sign that I'm about to fall apart.

I still get tight sometimes. Everyone does — you can watch it happen to professionals on the biggest stages. But I don't read it as proof that I'm not built for the moment anymore. Pressure is just what it feels like when something matters, and learning to keep playing anyway might be the most useful thing sports has ever taught me — on the court and completely off it.
