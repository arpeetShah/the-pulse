The Truth About Getting Recruited That Nobody Told Me

By The Pulse Founder  ·  6 min read

🏆

Most high schoolers who play a sport at any serious level have a vague idea that college recruitment exists and a very unclear idea of how it actually works. What I found when I started researching this is that recruitment is essentially a sales process — and the students who understand that get recruited at dramatically higher rates than equally talented athletes who don't.

The first thing you need to know is the timeline. Most people assume coaches find you. Sometimes they do — but not until junior or senior year, and by then many roster spots are already committed. The programs that recruit the way people imagine — scouts at games, out-of-nowhere phone calls — are Division I programs with large recruiting budgets. They represent a small fraction of college sports. The other 85% of college athletic programs are Division II, Division III, NAIA, and junior college, and virtually all of them recruit reactively. They respond to athletes who reach out. If you don't reach out, you don't exist.

"The biggest mistake recruited athletes make is waiting to be discovered. The process works the other way: you find the programs, you contact them directly, and you make it easy for coaches to say yes."

The practical steps are more straightforward than the mythology suggests. Build a highlight film — coaches watch the first sixty seconds and decide whether to keep watching, so put your best moments first. Create an athletic resume: your stats, GPA, test scores, graduation year, contact information, and a link to your film. Then identify programs at the right level — honest self-assessment here matters more than ego — and email the coach directly. Not the athletic department. The coach.

Your email should be short. One paragraph about who you are, one sentence about why you're interested in their specific program (do the research), and a link to your film. That's it. A coach reading a hundred recruiting emails will spend three seconds on yours — make those three seconds count. A long email reads as someone who doesn't understand how this works.

The GPA matters more than most athletes admit. Academic scholarships can be combined with athletic interest at the Division III level, where there are no athletic scholarships, to produce a genuinely good financial package. A student-athlete with a 3.7 GPA has more total options than a student-athlete with a 2.9, even if the athletic talent is identical. The academic piece isn't separate from recruiting — it's part of your profile.

Follow up. One email rarely gets a response. Two or three, spaced a few weeks apart, is normal and expected. Coaches are busy. They're not ignoring you — they're running a program, dealing with current athletes, and managing a hundred other things. A polite follow-up is professional, not desperate. The athletes who get responses are often just the ones who didn't stop after the first email.
